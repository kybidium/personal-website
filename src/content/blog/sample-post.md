I was chatting with a great friend recently about Instagram, and how it's a story-telling platform. People host picture books on their profiles for others to flip through, and these picture books generally tell stories about their lives and identities. People also share "stories" that serve as filler dialogue in their portfolios. If it's really good dialogue, they'll save it as a highlight for others to revisit these moments, as if it were a clip in a movie or TV show.

Some stories are obvious:
* *"I was going for a walk yesterday and I found a necklace on the ground."*
* *"A buddy of mine almost died in a skiing accident yesterday"*

Others, less so:
* *"A man was convicted of shoplifting at the Walgreens on Main Street last Saturday."*
* *"Five graduated cylinders were filled with 10 mL of 11M hydrochloric acid"*

We don't often think of these stories as stories. But what distinguishes the latter stories from the former is authority. The shoplifting story is the kind we'd hear from the news, and our trust in journalism confers authority upon its stories. The story about the experiment is the kind we'd read in a research paper, and if it's peer reviewed, then we grant the authority of scientific experts. The exception to this is when we ourselves witness whatever the story is about. Even then, we might disagree on how the story ought to be told. 
* *"Actually, it was 10.1 mL."*
* *"No, I could have sworn it was the Walgreens on Maple Street."*

A story starts with observations, real or fictitious. These observations are woven together to create an impression of something that happened, creating a memory of an event the audience never experienced. This relates more broadly to memory's constant reconstruction--the deeper layer is that memory itself is imagination, so imagining a story is not so far from remembering one of your own.

Replicability in science is a claim that if you reenact the story researchers have told you, you'll end up with the same results. The more people attest to this, the more the story told in research is taken seriously, because we generally believe in things the more others believe in them. And, the more others believe in institutions, the more credible they become, and thus the more credible their works become. This is *consensus*, the twin sister of authority.

Authority and consensus work hand in hand. Authority derives from the consent of others, and if many people consent to authority then there is a consensus on it among them. People have a tendency to believe in the consensus, because they look to others' perspectives to supplement their own. We look unfavorably upon those who don't--psychopaths, some call them. Deviants. Narcissists. These labels accuse their recipients of refusing to see eye-to-eye with everyone else. We perceive people who violate social norms as mentally ill--today, we use terms like "personality disorder" to refer to personalities that are not ordered as we'd like them to be. It's also worth noting how much these terms have changed over time--where psychopathy was once deemed "moral insanity," the term has been sanitized and anchored on concepts of empathy. "Moral insanity" is more honest, though--we're still casting judgement upon those who behave differently than we'd like them too. They're literally anti-social.

And- see- that's what a psychopath really is. It's not some sicko--it's a man who goes against the *fickle mores of the world around him.*


Authority and consensus, by the way, are etymologically begging you to see what I mean. Authority is the status of being an author. Consent is the verb of consensus. We let people author stories when we feel, together, that they ought to.
